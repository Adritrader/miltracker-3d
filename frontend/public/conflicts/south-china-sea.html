Overview

The South China Sea is a semi-enclosed body of water bounded by China, Taiwan, the Philippines, Malaysia, Brunei, Indonesia, Vietnam, and Singapore. It carries an estimated $3–5 trillion in annual trade, contains significant hydrocarbon reserves, and is home to some of the world's most productive fishing grounds. Multiple nations maintain overlapping and incompatible territorial claims to its waters, reefs, and islands — making it one of the most legally contested maritime regions on earth.

China's claim to approximately 90% of the sea, based on the so-called "nine-dash line" inherited from the Republic of China, was ruled incompatible with the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) by an international arbitration tribunal in July 2016. China rejected the ruling. The fundamental disagreement between China's historic claim and the rights of other coastal states under UNCLOS has never been resolved and continues to generate confrontations between Chinese forces and neighbouring states on an almost weekly basis.

Chinese Island Construction

China's most significant strategic move in the South China Sea has been an unprecedented artificial island construction programme in the Spratly and Paracel Island chains. Between 2013 and 2016, China dredged approximately 3,200 acres of artificial land onto previously submerged reefs, creating new islands large enough to support military facilities. The three largest — Fiery Cross Reef, Mischief Reef, and Subi Reef — each have:

  • Military-grade runways of approximately 3,000 metres, capable of handling all Chinese military aircraft including H-6 bombers, J-11/J-16 fighters, and Y-8/Y-9 ISR aircraft.
  • Large hangars capable of sheltering 24 aircraft each.
  • SAM (surface-to-air missile) launcher systems including HHQ-9B long-range air defence missiles.
  • Anti-ship cruise missile batteries.
  • Over-the-horizon radar systems and electronic intelligence collection facilities.
  • Deep-water harbours capable of hosting PLAN warships including guided-missile destroyers and submarines.

These facilities dramatically extend China's ability to project military power across the South China Sea, effectively giving the PLA a persistent military presence in disputed waters more than 1,000 kilometres from mainland China.

Philippines: Scarborough Shoal & Second Thomas Shoal

The Philippines has been at the forefront of confrontations with China in the South China Sea. Two locations have been particularly contentious:

Scarborough Shoal (Bajo de Masinloc) — A traditional Filipino fishing ground occupied by China Coast Guard vessels since 2012, following a standoff during which China failed to withdraw as agreed. Chinese vessels have used water cannons and physical obstructions to prevent Filipino fishermen from accessing waters they have used for generations. The shoal lies well within the Philippines' Exclusive Economic Zone under UNCLOS.

Second Thomas Shoal (Ayungin Shoal) — The site of the BRP Sierra Madre, a deliberately grounded Philippine Navy ship that serves as a forward operating base and assertion of Philippine sovereignty. China Coast Guard and People's Armed Forces Maritime Militia (PAFMM) vessels have repeatedly attempted to block Philippine resupply missions using water cannons, laser weapons (temporarily blinding Filipino sailors), and physical ramming. The confrontations have escalated significantly since 2023 and represent the most active point of ongoing friction between Manila and Beijing.

The Philippines under President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. has strengthened the US–Philippines alliance, granted the US military access to four additional bases under the Enhanced Defence Cooperation Agreement (EDCA), and conducted joint US–Philippine military exercises including carrier strike group transit exercises through the South China Sea.

During exercises or sensitive operations, PLAN vessels may operate under EMCON. US Navy carrier strike groups and P-8A Poseidon maritime patrol aircraft operating in the region are regularly visible via AIS and ADS-B respectively.

US Freedom of Navigation Operations

The United States Navy conducts regular Freedom of Navigation Operations (FONOPs) in the South China Sea, explicitly challenging what Washington considers excessive Chinese maritime claims. US warships, typically Arleigh Burke-class guided-missile destroyers, transit within 12 nautical miles of Chinese-occupied features while broadcasting AIS signals — making them visible on LiveWar3D — asserting that UNCLOS provides no territorial sea rights to artificial structures built on submerged reefs.

China invariably protests these transits as "provocations" and responds by scrambling warships and aircraft to shadow the US vessels. The interactions are typically tense but remain below the threshold of armed confrontation, though unsafe intercepts by Chinese aircraft and vessels have occurred on multiple occasions. The US Navy also conducts regular carrier strike group transits of the South China Sea with multiple warships as a show of force.

Vietnam, Malaysia, and Brunei

Vietnam has the longest history of disputes with China in the South China Sea. Vietnamese fishing boats are regularly harassed by Chinese vessels in waters Vietnam considers its own EEZ, and Chinese drilling rigs have been deployed in contested zones. Vietnam maintains a significant military presence in the Spratly Islands, occupying more than 20 features and constructing runways and port facilities, though all are smaller than China's artificial island bases.

Malaysia's state oil company Petronas operates drilling rigs in waters claimed by both Malaysia under UNCLOS and China under the nine-dash line, with Chinese vessels conducting regular patrols of the area. Brunei's small exclusive economic zone overlaps with Chinese claims in the southern Spratly Islands. Both nations have generally pursued quieter diplomatic approaches than the Philippines, though they conduct their own bilateral discussions with China.

Strategic Significance

Control of the South China Sea represents a strategic objective of the first order for China. A Chinese-dominated South China Sea would:

  • Give the PLAN a secure operating area for nuclear ballistic missile submarines (SSBNs) whose patrols underpin China's second-strike nuclear deterrent.
  • Allow China to interdict maritime trade flows to regional states including Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and the Philippines in a conflict scenario.
  • Provide logistical staging infrastructure for projecting military power further into the Pacific and Indian Oceans.
  • Establish a precedent that China's domestic law supersedes UNCLOS — with implications for Chinese claims in other maritime areas including the East China Sea.

For the United States, maintaining freedom of navigation in the South China Sea is both a legal commitment (as a guarantor of UNCLOS norms, despite not having ratified the treaty) and a strategic necessity for the global trade and alliance network upon which US global influence depends. The trajectory of this dispute over the coming decade will substantially shape whether the international rules-based order survives in the Indo-Pacific.
