Red Sea Crisis:
Houthi Attacks & Naval Operations

Updated: July 2026 ● Active Operations Source: Public OSINT

Overview

The Red Sea maritime crisis that began in late 2023 represents the most significant disruption to global shipping since the Suez Crisis of 1956. Yemen's Houthi movement (formally Ansar Allah), backed by Iran, launched an unprecedented campaign of anti-ship ballistic missile, cruise missile, and drone attacks against commercial vessels and naval warships transiting the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden, citing the attacks as solidarity with Palestinians in Gaza.

The Bab el-Mandeb Strait — the narrow chokepoint at the southern end of the Red Sea connecting it to the Gulf of Aden and ultimately to the Indian Ocean — carries approximately 15% of global maritime trade and 8% of world seaborne oil. The Houthi campaign has effectively closed this route to vessels judged to have Israeli connections or to fly the flags of nations the Houthis consider complicit in the Gaza conflict, with the definition expanding over time to include almost any US or European-flagged vessel.

Houthi Weapons Systems

Ansar Allah has deployed a diverse arsenal of weapons acquired primarily from Iran:

  • Anti-Ship Ballistic Missiles (ASBMs) — Including the Tankil and Asef variants, capable of delivering warheads at terminal velocities that challenge existing naval air defences. These have caused significant damage to several vessels.
  • Anti-Ship Cruise Missiles — Al-Mandab-1 and Iranian-supplied variants that skim the sea surface at low altitude to avoid radar detection. Cruise missiles have been the most frequently used weapon type in the campaign.
  • One-Way Attack Drones (UAVs) — Samad-series and Iranian Shahed-derived designs used in both individual attacks and saturation salvos designed to overwhelm point defence systems. These are relatively cheap ($10,000–50,000 each) and easily produced or imported in volume.
  • Naval Mines — Floating contact mines have been reported in shipping lanes, creating a persistent threat to vessels not actively targeted by other systems.
  • Anti-Ship Missiles (ASMs) — Iranian-supplied C-802 and derivative missiles have been used against specific vessels, including the attack on the MV Tutor that resulted in crew casualties.

Coalition Naval Response

The United States established Operation Prosperity Guardian in December 2023 as a multinational naval escort coalition, subsequently complemented by the parallel EU Operation Aspides established in early 2024. The two operations have different mandates: Prosperity Guardian conducts proactive strikes against Houthi launch sites in Yemen, while Aspides focuses exclusively on defensive intercepts of incoming missiles and drones targeting commercial vessels.

Key naval assets deployed:

  • USS Dwight D. Eisenhower (CVN-69) — Aircraft carrier deployed to the Red Sea region in late 2023 through mid-2024, conducting strikes on Houthi targets. Multiple carrier strike groups have rotated through the AOR.
  • Arleigh Burke-class Destroyers — USS Gravely, USS Carney, USS Mason, USS Theodore Roosevelt and others have intercepted dozens of Houthi missiles and drones using SM-2 and SM-6 air defence missiles. The SM-6 intercepts of ballistic missiles represented some of the first operational uses of this capability in combat.
  • HMS Diamond (D34) — Royal Navy Type 45 Daring-class destroyer, conducted multiple successful Sea Viper (Aster 30) intercepts of Houthi drones.
  • FS Languedoc (D646) — French Navy Forbin-class destroyer, operational under EU Aspides.
  • Ticonderoga-class Cruisers — USS Philippine Sea provided additional Aegis air defence capability during its deployment.
  • SSBN/SSN submarines — US submarines have launched Tomahawk cruise missiles against Houthi radar sites, missile storage facilities, and launch infrastructure in Yemen.
AIS Tracking Note: US Navy destroyers operating in the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden frequently have their AIS transponders active, making their positions visible on LiveWar3D. Periods of EMCON (emissions control) during active operations will cause them to disappear from the tracking plot. Commercial vessels that have been targeted or threatened by Houthis are also visible; several targeted vessels have transmitted distress messages that appear in GDELT news data pinned to the globe.

Economic Impact

The diversion of shipping around the Cape of Good Hope — adding 10–14 days to Europe–Asia voyages — has had significant consequences for global trade:

  • Container shipping rates between Asia and Europe increased by 200–400% at peak disruption periods in early 2024.
  • Major carriers including Maersk, MSC, CMA CGM, Hapag-Lloyd, and Evergreen suspended transits through the Red Sea, diverting an estimated 70–80% of container traffic via the Cape.
  • The Suez Canal Authority reported a 40–50% drop in vessel transits and associated toll revenue.
  • LNG and oil tanker operators faced higher insurance premiums and operational costs, contributing to energy price volatility in European markets.
  • Just-in-time supply chains for European automotive, electronics, and retail sectors experienced delays and inventory disruptions.

US and Allied Strikes on Yemen

The United States and United Kingdom conducted coordinated strikes on Houthi military infrastructure in Yemen beginning January 2024, targeting radar installations, missile storage facilities, launch sites, and command infrastructure. These strikes, while degrading some Houthi capability, failed to stop the maritime campaign — the Houthis proved resilient, dispersing assets and continuing attacks despite significant attrition.

Israel conducted retaliatory strikes on the Houthi-controlled port of Hodeidah following Houthi long-range drone and missile attacks on Israeli territory, targeting fuel storage and port infrastructure. These strikes caused fires visible on NASA FIRMS satellite imagery on the LiveWar3D platform.

Context: The Yemen Civil War

The Houthi Red Sea campaign cannot be understood without the context of the broader Yemen civil war, which began in 2014–2015 when Ansar Allah forces captured the capital Sanaa, prompting a Saudi Arabia–led coalition intervention in March 2015. The conflict killed an estimated 150,000–377,000 people (including indirect deaths) and created one of the world's worst humanitarian crises. A UN-brokered ceasefire agreement in April 2022 largely held through 2023, reducing frontline fighting even as the Houthis consolidated control over most of Yemen's populated highlands.

The Red Sea campaign transformed the Houthis from a regional actor into a global one, demonstrating that a non-state armed group with Iranian-supplied precision weapons could credibly threaten international maritime commerce and contest naval superiority against the world's most advanced navies.
