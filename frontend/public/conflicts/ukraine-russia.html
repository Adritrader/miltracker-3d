Ukraine–Russia War:
Live Military Tracking & Intelligence

Updated: July 2026 ● Active Operations Source: Public OSINT

Overview

Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine, which began on 24 February 2022, has entered its fourth year as one of the largest conventional military conflicts in Europe since the Second World War. The conflict has evolved through distinct phases — the failed initial assault on Kyiv, the Donbas ground attrition campaign, Ukraine's 2022 counter-offensives in Kharkiv and Kherson, and the subsequent positional warfare along a roughly 1,000-kilometre front line that has defined the conflict since late 2023.

The war has reshaped European security architecture, accelerated NATO's eastern expansion (Finland and Sweden joined in 2023 and 2024 respectively), and triggered the largest mobilisation of Western military aid since the Cold War. Ukraine has received advanced Western weapons systems including HIMARS multiple-launch rocket systems, Leopard 2 and Abrams main battle tanks, F-16 Fighting Falcons, Storm Shadow and SCALP cruise missiles, Patriot and IRIS-T air defence systems, and billions of dollars in financial support from the United States and European Union.

Current Front Lines

Fighting in mid-2026 is concentrated along several active axes:

  • Donetsk Oblast — The longest and most contested stretch of the front, with Russian forces maintaining pressure around Pokrovsk and Kurakhove while Ukrainian forces conduct defensive operations. Artillery exchanges and drone strikes occur daily.
  • Zaporizhzhia — The southern front has seen periodic Russian probing attacks toward the city of Zaporizhzhia. The Zaporizhzhia Nuclear Power Plant, occupied by Russian forces since March 2022, remains a source of ongoing international concern.
  • Kharkiv Region — Russian forces crossed the border into northern Kharkiv Oblast in May 2024, forcing Ukrainian evacuations. Subsequent Ukrainian counter-operations partially stabilised the front.
  • Kursk Oblast (Russia) — Ukraine's surprise August 2024 cross-border incursion into Russian Kursk Oblast represented the first foreign military occupation of Russian territory since the Second World War, creating a secondary front that tied down Russian reserves.
OSINT Note: ADS-B transponder coverage near the Ukrainian front lines is limited. Military aircraft operating in active threat environments may disable transponders or use Mode S squawk codes that obscure their identity. The data visible on LiveWar3D reflects what cooperative tracking networks can observe — primarily aircraft operating from secure rear areas, NATO AWACS and ISR aircraft over Poland and Romania, and transit flights.

Air Operations

Russia's Aerospace Forces (VKS) have conducted the most intensive air campaign seen in Europe since the Second World War. Daily attacks using Shahed-136/131 loitering munitions, Kh-101 cruise missiles, Kalibr naval cruise missiles, Iskander-M ballistic missiles, and Kinzhal air-launched hypersonic missiles have targeted Ukrainian power generation, heating infrastructure, ammunition depots, rail networks, and military production facilities.

Ukraine's air defence network — built around a patchwork of Soviet-era S-300 systems, US-supplied Patriot and NASAMS batteries, German IRIS-T SLM launchers, and French SAMP/T — has achieved significant intercept rates against subsonic cruise missiles and drones, but struggles against ballistic and hypersonic threats due to limited interceptor inventories.

Ukraine's own air force has transitioned to F-16 Fighting Falcons supplied by Denmark, the Netherlands, Norway, and Belgium, phasing out older MiG-29 and Su-27 platforms. Ukrainian domestically produced Palianytsia and Neptune cruise missiles and Bober drones have struck targets hundreds of kilometres inside Russia, including oil refineries, ammunition storage facilities, radar installations, and airfields.

Naval Situation

Ukraine's Black Sea campaign has been one of the strategic surprises of the war. Using domestically developed naval drones (USDVs) and anti-ship missiles, Ukraine has sunk or severely damaged numerous Russian Black Sea Fleet vessels including the Moskva cruiser (flagship, April 2022), the Saratov landing ship, several patrol vessels, and the Rostov-on-Don submarine. Russian surface vessels have largely withdrawn from the western Black Sea, ceding the maritime domain to Ukraine for the purposes of the grain export initiative and coastal defence.

The Russian Black Sea Fleet, now based primarily at Novorossiysk following attacks on Sevastopol, maintains submarine operations and occasionally launches Kalibr cruise missile strikes from submerged positions. AIS data on this platform tracks civilian and naval vessels with active transponders in the Black Sea, Azov Sea, and surrounding waters.

International Military Support

The conflict has mobilised unprecedented international military support for Ukraine. Key contributors include:

  • United States — Largest single donor. HIMARS systems, Patriot batteries, Abrams M1A1 tanks, F-16s (via Denmark/Netherlands), ATACMS long-range missiles, artillery ammunition, and over $60 billion in combined military, financial, and humanitarian aid.
  • Germany — Leopard 2 tanks, IRIS-T air defence systems, Patriot batteries, artillery systems including PzH 2000 self-propelled howitzers.
  • United Kingdom — Challenger 2 tanks, Storm Shadow cruise missiles, AS-90 self-propelled guns, NLAW anti-tank systems.
  • France — CAESAR self-propelled howitzers, SCALP cruise missiles, Mirage 2000-5 fighters, AMX-10RC armoured vehicles.
  • Poland — Among the largest per-GDP donors; MiG-29 fighters, T-72 tanks, Piorun man-portable air defence systems, extensive logistics support.

Tracking Ukraine on LiveWar3D

LiveWar3D aggregates multiple open-source data streams relevant to the Ukraine conflict:

  • ADS-B Aircraft Tracking — NATO AWACS (E-3 Sentry) and ISR aircraft operating over Poland, Romania, and the Black Sea region are frequently visible. US Air Force RC-135 Rivet Joint intelligence-gathering aircraft, P-8 Poseidon maritime patrol aircraft, and tanker aircraft (KC-135, A330 MRTT) operating in the region broadcast ADS-B signals picked up by cooperative ground receiver networks.
  • AIS Ship Tracking — Cargo vessels transiting the Black Sea, naval support vessels, and occasionally warships with active AIS transponders are visible in the maritime view.
  • NASA FIRMS Fire Data — Satellite thermal anomalies corresponding to artillery strikes, missile impacts, and industrial fires in the conflict zone appear as fire hotspot pins on the globe, typically with a 3-6 hour delay from the FIRMS processing pipeline.
  • GDELT News Events — Breaking news reports from the Ukrainian conflict are geocoded and displayed as news pins on the globe, updated every 15 minutes.
  • Conflict Zone Overlays — Colour-coded danger zones indicate areas of active fighting based on ACLED conflict event data.
