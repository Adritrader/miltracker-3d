Overview

The Korean Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) — a 250-kilometre-long, roughly 4-kilometre-wide buffer strip established by the 1953 Korean Armistice Agreement — remains one of the most heavily fortified borders on Earth, separating North and South Korea along a line that has never been formalised into a peace treaty. More than seven decades after the armistice, the two Koreas remain technically at war, with over a million combined active-duty troops stationed within range of the border.

North Korea's continued development of ballistic missile and nuclear weapons capability, periodic artillery and drone incidents along the DMZ and Northern Limit Line (NLL) maritime boundary, and unpredictable leadership rhetoric keep the peninsula among the world's most closely monitored military flashpoints.

North Korean Missile Programme

North Korea has conducted an extensive series of ballistic and cruise missile tests, progressively expanding its arsenal:

  • Hwasong-series ICBMs — Intercontinental-range systems (Hwasong-17, Hwasong-18) demonstrated capability to reach the continental United States, tested on lofted trajectories to avoid violating neighbouring airspace.
  • Hwasong-11/KN-23 short-range ballistic missiles — Solid-fuel, manoeuvrable re-entry vehicle designs modelled on Russia's Iskander system, fired regularly toward the Sea of Japan (East Sea) and used to signal displeasure at joint US–South Korean exercises.
  • Hwasan-31 tactical nuclear warheads — North Korea has publicly displayed miniaturised warheads it claims fit its shorter-range systems, part of a stated doctrine of tactical nuclear use against South Korean and US forces in the event of conflict.
  • Submarine-launched ballistic missiles (SLBMs) — Tested from the Gorae-class experimental submarine, part of an effort to build a survivable second-strike capability.
  • Reconnaissance satellites — North Korea has conducted repeated satellite launch attempts, framed domestically as space programme development but viewed internationally as ballistic missile technology testing under UN sanctions.
OSINT Tracking Note: North Korean missile launches are typically detected by South Korean and Japanese early-warning radar and confirmed via GDELT news reporting, which appears as geocoded news pins on LiveWar3D. North Korea does not operate a cooperative ADS-B or AIS transponder network, so North Korean military aircraft and vessels are not directly visible on this platform — only allied response assets and confirmed missile-test news events are tracked.

US Forces Korea and Allied Posture

United States Forces Korea (USFK), numbering approximately 28,500 personnel, maintains a permanent presence anchored by the US Eighth Army, Seventh Air Force, and rotational naval assets. Camp Humphreys, south of Seoul, is the largest overseas US military installation in the world. Key allied capabilities include:

  • F-16 and F-35A squadrons — Based at Kunsan and Osan Air Bases, providing air superiority and strike capability integrated with South Korea's own F-35A fleet.
  • THAAD battery — Deployed to Seongju, South Korea, providing terminal-phase ballistic missile defence against North Korean short- and medium-range missiles, historically a point of friction with China.
  • Patriot PAC-3 batteries — Layered lower-tier missile defence integrated with South Korea's domestic KAMD (Korea Air and Missile Defense) system.
  • Carrier strike group visits — US Navy carrier strike groups periodically transit to South Korean ports as a show of force following North Korean provocations.
  • B-1B and B-52H bomber flyovers — Strategic bomber deployments from Guam are a recurring signal of extended deterrence following North Korean tests.

Joint Military Exercises

South Korea and the United States conduct large-scale joint exercises throughout the year — including Freedom Shield (spring) and Ulchi Freedom Shield (summer) — involving tens of thousands of troops and integrating air, ground, naval, and cyber components. North Korea routinely condemns these exercises as rehearsals for invasion and has historically timed missile tests, artillery drills, or rhetorical escalations to coincide with them. Japan has increasingly participated in trilateral exercises with South Korea and the United States, reflecting deepening security cooperation in response to North Korean and Chinese activity in the region.

Northern Limit Line and Naval Incidents

The disputed maritime boundary in the Yellow Sea (West Sea), the Northern Limit Line, has been the site of repeated naval clashes, including the 2010 sinking of the ROKS Cheonan and the shelling of Yeonpyeong Island. South Korean and US naval forces maintain a persistent presence in the area, and AIS-tracked South Korean naval vessels and cooperative merchant traffic are visible on LiveWar3D where transponders remain active.
